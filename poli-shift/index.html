Framing the Narrative:
Ideological Mimicry in Large Language Models

Political stance in LLMs is interaction-dependent.

Poli‑SHIFT is a dataset and evaluation framework for testing whether signals about the user's political stance systematically move the political stance an LLM expresses.

Olivia Macmillan‑Scott · Michael Jacobs · Nils Metternich · Mirco Musolesi

Three experimental conditions

Same model. Different framing. Different stance.

Poli‑SHIFT holds the underlying policy question fixed and varies one interaction signal at a time. Pick a treatment, then pick a variant, to see the typical direction a model's stance moves (averaged across the open-weight models).

Stance is scored on a 5-point scale, where higher means more pro-side. “Pro-side” and “anti-side” are the two ends of each issue, such as supporting or opposing military aid to Ukraine.

Headline results

Political framing is directional, not arbitrary.

Both contested terminology and premise framing move the same model's stance in a consistent direction, for every one of the ten models, open-weight and closed-source alike. Premise framing produces the larger average shift.

Contested terminology

Pro-side vs. anti-side wording, by model. Dot = mean; whisker = 95% CI. Contested terms can carry different presuppositions, so this shows sensitivity to politically loaded wording. Among open-weight models, 24.0% of matched MCQ pairs and 9.8% of open-text pairs cross the neutral midpoint (16.9% overall; 4.2% for closed-source models).

Closed-source models show the same pattern, with smaller reversals.

All 12 framing effects point in the same direction as for open-weight models, but terminology reversals are rarer, open-text scoring is less reliable, and refusals are more common.

Paper summary

Key findings.

Political wording changes LLM responses. Across ten models, using terminology associated with opposing sides of a political debate systematically shifts the political stance expressed, even when the underlying policy question stays the same.

Wording can reverse expressed political support. Among the seven open-weight models, this happens in 24.0% of matched multiple-choice pairs and 9.8% of open-text pairs. Reversals are less frequent among the three closed-source models.

Political assumptions matter too. Questions built around opposing political premises produce even larger differences in responses, although these differences also reflect the distinct claims and assumptions presented.

Models adapt to users' stated politics. Describing a user as liberal or conservative consistently shifts responses in the corresponding direction. Political ideology has a much stronger effect than the other biographical characteristics tested.

Political stance is not fixed. The position an LLM expresses depends partly on how a question is framed and what it knows about the user, suggesting that political evaluations should measure this sensitivity alongside baseline bias.
